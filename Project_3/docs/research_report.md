# Make the judge check what's missing

The judge improves most when it stops reading the reply as a whole and starts checking it against the policy, condition by condition. The baseline catches only **6.7% of dropped conditions and 50% of hedge removals**, while it catches 80–100% of wrong numbers and wrong policy details. That is the same pattern the literature reports. Omissions and weakened qualifiers leave nothing in the reply for the model to notice. Reference-guided checking that makes the missing item explicit is the best-documented fix: in FBI, GPT-4-turbo's detection of a removed fact rose from 6% to 48% once it was given a reference, and in AbsenceBench, adding explicit placeholders for removed content raised Claude's F1 from 66.9 to 83.3. The recommended recipe has four parts. First, a deterministic number rule that validates itself on the labelled examples. Second, one structured Haiku call that pulls out every commitment in the reply, finds the policy rule it relies on, and checks that rule's conditions and hedges against the reply. Third, plain code that turns those fields into a verdict. Fourth, a verify call that runs only on suspected fails, with a third call to break ties. This keeps within 3 calls, holds no state, and contains nothing specific to any criterion. The criterion and its examples are just text passed in at runtime. Run-to-run consistency comes from temperature 0, short structured reasoning, verdicts computed in code, and deterministic fallbacks. It must not come from a verdict cache, which very likely breaks the no-state rule and games the consistency metric.

## The baseline fails exactly where published judges fail

Our starter-pack baseline scores **0.697 combined (macro catch 0.592, macro keep 0.847, kappa 0.531)** on 226 dev pairs. Catch by edit type is: condition_removal 0.067, hedge_removal 0.500, wrong_number 0.800, wrong_policy_detail 1.000. Keep is 0.733 for sentence_reorder, 0.800 for filler_padding, 0.833 for translation, 0.867 for polite_opener and 1.000 for padding_removal. Correct, unedited replies pass only 85.8% of the time (our analysis, not from the notes). Because catch is macro-averaged, each edit type counts equally. Condition removal alone costs about 0.23 of macro catch, so it is the biggest single lever in the score.

This profile matches the most relevant paper in the brief almost exactly. "A Judge Should Know What Changed" (arXiv 2608.24419) tested 7 judges. They were highly invariant to construct-preserving edits (**S≈0.945**) but only weakly sensitive to construct-changing ones (**R≈0.319**). Judges reacted more to "scope" edits than to "strength" edits, and that gap (+0.121) held for every judge. **Hedge removal was the least-detected edit of all (0.210)**, and condition removal was also low (0.379) ([arXiv 2608.24419](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24419)). The paper also tested **claude-haiku-4.5 and found it the most sensitive of the 7 judges (R=0.561, S=0.917)**. Our remaining weakness is therefore built into single-pass judging and is not a matter of model choice; switching models was never an option anyway. One mismatch stands out: in that paper, reordering flips verdicts only 8.4% of the time, but our baseline false-fails 27% of reordered replies. That points to something in our prompt or data. One plausible cause is a reorder that moves a condition away from the promise it qualifies, which the judge then misreads. This is an inference and has not been tested.

FBI, the EMNLP 2024 blind-spot benchmark, gives the clearest numbers on omissions. A single-answer GPT-4-turbo judge caught **only 6% of "Remove Fact" perturbations**, compared with 30% of number errors and 43% of opposite facts. With a reference answer, detection rose to **48%, 92% and 100%** respectively ([FBI](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13439)). FBI also found that **rubric-style prompting made things worse**: 0.73 of factual errors went unpenalized versus 0.54 with a plain prompt. Explanations sometimes named the error without lowering the score. AbsenceBench gives the mechanism: "transformer attention mechanisms cannot easily attend to 'gaps' in documents" ([AbsenceBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11440)). Standard faithfulness checkers only check reply → reference, so by design they cannot see a dropped "if the car is not reserved" ([MiniCheck](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10774v2)). NLI research shows quantifiers and modality are long-standing weak spots ([EQUATE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.03735), [natural-logic quantifiers](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.05034v3)).

Keep has its own failure modes. In an 18-model contact-center study, changing only politeness, formality or style flipped binary QA verdicts **6.9–7.9%** of the time, and explicit fairness instructions helped only modestly. For a Haiku model (3.5), they made results slightly worse ([Observe.AI, arXiv 2602.14970](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14970v1)). Rubric wording alone predicts much of a judge's label: when the response was reversed, the judge flipped the right way only **37.7%** of the time ([arXiv 2609.02942](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02942)). Our 14% false-fail rate on correct replies is consistent with a criterion like "over-promise?" priming the judge to look for something to fail.

## Making the absence explicit beats more reasoning

The strongest lever in the literature is to **turn "notice what is missing" into "check an item on a list."** The judge never sees the original reply, but it does see the policy, and the policy can serve as the list of key facts. FineSurE checks completeness this way: it extracts key facts from the source and aligns each one to the summary ([FineSurE](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00908v3)). MED-OMIT does the same for clinical omissions ([MED-OMIT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08303v1)). AbsenceBench's placeholder result (+16 to +22 F1 for strong models) shows the size of the gain when the gap is made visible ([AbsenceBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11440)). For us this means a two-way check on each commitment in the reply. Reply → policy asks whether the commitment is supported and whether its number is no more generous than the policy's. Policy → reply asks whether every condition and hedge attached to the matching policy rule survives in the reply. The second check must be scoped carefully. A condition counts as "dropped" only when the reply states the entitlement the condition qualifies, never just because the reply doesn't mention an unrelated rule. That is what protects keep on padding removal and shorter replies.

How the extraction is structured matters more than how fine-grained it is. MiniCheck found that splitting text into claims changed GPT-4's accuracy by just +0.3 points while costing 2–4x more ([MiniCheck](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10774v2)). Granularity research warns that atomic facts lose the context needed to verify them ([TriQua](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.05228)). Claimify extracts claims so that critical context stays with each claim ([Claimify](https://preview.aclanthology.org/landing_page/2025.acl-long.348.pdf)). Atomic facts or RefChecker-style triplets would strip exactly the "if…" and "usually" we need ([RefChecker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14486)). The extraction schema should keep qualifiers as **named fields on each claim**, not split them off. Hedges need their own explicit comparison. No benchmark targets "usually → always", so the prompt should require an ordinal hedge strength for both the policy rule and the reply claim: guaranteed/always > usually/typically > may/subject to availability. The model should normalize hedges in other languages ("normalerweise", "généralement", "di solito", "normalmente") onto that scale.

Free-form chain-of-thought adds little once criteria and references are detailed. On BIGGEN-Bench with full criteria and references, direct and CoT judging were essentially tied (α 0.912 vs 0.908) ([Yamauchi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13639v1)). DynaGuard's policy-compliance judge gained about 1.3 F1 from CoT. Its biggest error class was **multi-clause rules (60.7% error rate)**, which is our "entitlement plus condition" structure ([DynaGuard](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02563v3)). RocketEval argues that small judges are "not easily enhanced" by CoT, and that checklists help them instead ([RocketEval](https://www.iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/27674)). Checklists themselves are mixed evidence. CheckEval raised inter-judge agreement sharply ([CheckEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.18771v2)), and TICK added +5.8 points of exact agreement ([TICK](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03608v1)). But an EMNLP 2025 study found no significant gain for direct scoring, and judged about 40% of auto-generated checklist items harmful ([Furuhashi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15218v1)). The structure to use is therefore a **short, fixed, policy-anchored set of comparison fields**, not a long generated rubric.

Deterministic rules are where we have our own hard data. The rule "fail if the reply contains any digit-number absent from policy.md" **caught 35/35 wrong_number edits and false-failed 0 of the other 417 dev replies**. Two cases slip through: a generous number that happens to appear elsewhere in the policy, and numbers written as words. The LLM call must still cover those. Practitioner reports agree that verbatim number matching catches most numeric errors ([YLD](https://www.yld.com/blog/evaluating-llm-summaries-at-scale-what-we-tried-and-what-worked)). They also warn that such a rule over-flags correctly derived numbers, such as totals or "1 day" for "24 hours" ([Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/why-my-llm-guardrail-flagged-the-right-answers-and-why-i-refused-to-fix-it-0db77efb0644)). Hidden cases may contain those derived numbers even though dev did not. To stay criterion-agnostic, the rule should validate itself in `prepare()`. It runs over the provided labelled examples (and the dev pairs when they share the criterion). If it fires on any harmless or pass example, it is disabled. Under a new criterion where it has fired on no fail example, it is demoted to a hint passed to the LLM rather than a verdict.

Few-shot examples help, but they are fragile. Performance shifts with label balance, order and count, and many-shot examples with rationales beat examples without them ([Song et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11629v6); [Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/llm-evaluators/)). Practitioner guidance is to include clear pass, clear fail and borderline examples, and to put the critique before the verdict ([evals-skills](https://skills.sh/hamelsmu/evals-skills/write-judge-prompt)). Dropbox warns against copying example keywords into the prompt ([Dropbox](https://dropbox.tech/machine-learning/optimizing-dropbox-dash-relevance-judge-with-dspy)). For the surprise criterion, use every provided example, in fixed order with labels alternating, each with a one-line rationale. Frame them as illustrations of the principle, so the judge generalizes to the hidden, unseen edit types rather than pattern-matching surface cues.

## A three-call pipeline whose verdict lives in code

The design puts judgment where the model is strong (reading, quoting, comparing two short texts) and puts the decision in code, where it is deterministic. The verify call spends budget only on suspected fails. That is where keep is lost, and it is also where our 14% false-fail rate on originals comes from. The pipeline is the same for any criterion. Only the criterion text, the examples and the self-validated rule flags change.

```
prepare(criterion, examples, policy, dev_pairs):         # once per criterion, no LLM needed
    rules = [number_not_in_policy, ...]                   # generic, text-level
    for r in rules: enable r only if it fires on 0 pass/harmless examples
                    (mode = "verdict" if it also fires on >=1 fail example, else "hint")
    freeze prompt text: policy + criterion + examples (fixed order, alternating labels)

judge(reply):                                             # stateless; <=3 calls
    hits = [r(reply, policy) for r in enabled rules]
    if any(h.mode == "verdict" for h in hits): return FAIL          # 0 LLM calls

    # CALL 1 (T=0): extract + verify, both directions, one JSON object
    j1 = llm(prompt_extract, reply, hints=hits)
    #   items[]: reply_quote, english_gloss, relevant_to_criterion, policy_rule_quote|null,
    #            reply_number, policy_number, number_direction(same|more_generous|stricter|na),
    #            policy_conditions[] -> each {condition, status: kept|dropped|contradicted|na},
    #            policy_hedge_strength, reply_hedge_strength (0-3 ordinal),
    #            support: supported|contradicted|unsupported_extra|no_claim
    #   criterion_violation_notes, verdict_hint (last field)
    flags = code_aggregate(j1)    # FAIL-candidates: contradicted, more_generous, any condition
                                  # dropped on an asserted entitlement, reply_hedge > policy_hedge,
                                  # unsupported_extra commitment
    if not flags: return PASS

    # CALL 2 (T=0): verify only the flagged items, fresh context (no call-1 reasoning)
    j2 = llm(prompt_verify, reply, flagged_items)   # per item: real_violation | harmless_rewording
    if all confirmed or all rejected and agrees_with_call1_strength(...): return decided verdict
    # CALL 3 (T=0): tiebreak on disagreement, alternative framing (holistic, with examples)
    return majority(call1_flags, call2, llm(prompt_holistic, reply))
```

Two alternatives are worth testing against this default. Variant B splits call 1 into a precision call (reply → policy) and a separate recall call (policy → reply's conditions and hedges), keeping the verify call. This follows FineSurE's separate completeness pass. It may raise condition catch, but it gives up the tiebreak. Variant C drops verification and runs three differently framed calls at T=0 (structured, holistic with examples, and adversarial "find any commitment stronger than the policy") with a majority vote. That is a same-model take on PoLL panel diversity ([PoLL](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18796v2)). The verify step needs one guardrail that is still criterion-agnostic. The verify prompt must state the general asymmetry, "**stating a conditional entitlement unconditionally is stronger than the reference and counts as a change in meaning**", or call 2 will talk itself out of real omissions as "harmless brevity." Measure how much catch the verify call loses, not just how much keep it recovers.

### Prompt layout for Haiku 4.5

Anthropic's guidance for Haiku 4.5 covers placement, structure and examples. Put long documents first and the query last; this improved quality by "up to 30 percent" in their tests. Wrap each content type in XML tags. Use 3–5 examples in `<example>` tags ([Anthropic prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). The user message should therefore run in this order: `<policy>`, then `<criterion>`, then `<examples>`, then the customer question if one is provided, then `<reply lang="xx">`, then instructions. The system message holds the role and the output contract. This order also keeps the static prefix cacheable if a gateway ever supports caching, though Anthropic's OpenAI-compatible layer does not ([OpenAI SDK compatibility](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/openai-sdk)).

Put the invariance principle in the instructions, worded so it stays criterion-agnostic: "judge only what the criterion asks about; changes in greeting, filler, order, length or language matter only if the criterion itself is about them." A surprise criterion could be about tone, so "openers never matter" must not be hard-coded. Evidence fields come before the verdict field. Each item quotes the original-language span and adds a short English gloss. Reasoning stays short; extended thinking would add about 10 s per call and its output is not returned through the compat layer ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/claude-4-5-haiku-reasoning/providers)).

For output, `response_format` accepts only `json_schema` with `strict: true`; `json_object` returns a 400 error. `seed` and `logprobs` are ignored, and `n` must be 1 ([OpenAI SDK compatibility](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/openai-sdk)). Smoke-test the hackathon gateway once. If strict schemas don't pass through, fall back to tagged JSON or XML with a regex parse that takes the last match. Treat `finish_reason == "length"` as a parse failure, and make every parse failure produce a fixed default verdict rather than a retry with a different outcome.

### Determinism and multilingual handling

Temperature 0 reduces flips but does not eliminate them. Batch-size variation on inference servers is enough to change outputs: 1,000 completions at T=0 gave 80 unique outputs in one experiment ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). A 2026 grader study found Haiku 4.5 flipping borderline items at the same rate as Sonnet even under greedy decoding ([arXiv 2606.26185](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.26185.md)). Haiku 4.5 still accepts `temperature: 0` through the compat layer. It is rejected only on the native 2026-10-21 API version ([migration notes](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/migration-2026-10-21)). Confirm that the gateway forwards it. Flips cluster at the decision boundary, so the levers that remain are decisive rules for edge cases and verdicts computed in code from extracted fields. A field such as "condition dropped: yes" is more stable than a holistic PASS/FAIL token.

For languages, keep the reply in its original language. Give the policy and criterion in English, and have the model reason in English while quoting the original span. Anthropic reports that the current Haiku keeps 97.4–98.1% of its English Global-MMLU score in DE/FR/ES/IT ([Anthropic multilingual](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/multilingual-support)). Cross-lingual judging is still inconsistent, with average Fleiss' κ ≈ 0.3 across 25 languages ([Fu & Liu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12201)), and judges favour text that reads like a translation ([arXiv 2603.10351](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.10351.md)). A separate translate-first call would therefore spend budget and add risk on exactly the modal verbs and numbers we care about. Number extraction in the rule pre-pass must handle locale formats ("1.000,50 €", "24 Stunden").

## Prove each piece on dev before 14:30

The schedule is fixed: the surprise criterion arrives around 14:30, code freezes at 16:00, and two runs per criterion on hidden cases finish by 16:45. So every design decision must be settled on dev before 14:30. The 90 minutes after that are for running the frozen code against the new examples, not for engineering.

Rate per-type numbers with care. Edit types hold roughly 15–35 items, so one item moves a type's rate by about 3–7 points. Run each configuration **twice** so that run-to-run agreement is measured alongside accuracy. One full dev pass is 452 replies at no more than 3 calls each, roughly 3–6 minutes at 12 workers given about 2.5–3 s per Haiku call ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/claude-4-5-haiku/providers)). Do not draw few-shot examples from the pairs used for scoring ([evals-skills](https://skills.sh/hamelsmu/evals-skills/write-judge-prompt)).

| Step | Config | Question answered |
|---|---|---|
| A0 | Baseline (twice) | Noise floor; run-to-run disagreement rate |
| A1 | A0 + number rule | Does the rule's dev result (35/35, 0 false fails) hold in the pipeline? |
| A2 | Call 1 structured extraction, code aggregation, no verify | Catch gain on condition and hedge removal; keep cost |
| A3 | A2 without policy → reply condition fields | Isolates the omission check |
| A4 | A2 + verify-on-fail | Keep recovered vs catch lost |
| A5 | A4 + tiebreak call | Consistency gain worth the third call? |
| A6 | Variant B (separate recall call) and Variant C (3-framing vote) | Best use of the budget |
| A7 | Few-shot with rationales vs without; JSON schema vs XML tags | Prompt-format choices |
| A8 | Leave-one-edit-type-out | Generalization to unseen edit types |
| A9 | Mock surprise criteria (write 1–2 with 4–6 labelled examples each, including one not about policy numbers) | Does `prepare()` disable or demote rules correctly, and does the code run unchanged? |

Choose one generic knob (for example, how many votes it takes to fail, or whether verify can overturn rule hits) by the harmonic mean on held-out dev, never per criterion. Rehearse the 14:30 handoff end to end at least once. Load the new criterion and examples, run `prepare()`, check pass rates on the provided examples, and launch a full run with incremental writes, retries with backoff that honour `retry-after`, and logging of `response.model`. Haiku 4.5 is now a legacy model past its "not sooner than October 15, 2026" retirement date, so a gateway silently swapping the model is a real risk ([Haiku 4.5 overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/haiku-4-5/overview)).

## Risks and what not to do

**Do not use a verdict cache keyed on an input hash in the submitted runs.** One research note calls it "the single biggest consistency lever." The hackathon forbids state between calls, and the consistency metric exists to measure the judge's real run-to-run stability. The starter's own docs say not to set `JUDGE_LLM_CACHE` when measuring consistency. A cache would very likely break the rules and inflate the consistency score artificially. It is acceptable only for local development speed and must be switched off for all four scored runs. The same logic rules out anything that carries information from one reply's judgment to another's, such as adaptive few-shot selection based on earlier verdicts or running counters.

The next risk is **hiding criterion-specific logic** in code. Regexes for "cancellation", hard-coded hedge lists used as verdicts, or branches on edit-type names all break the requirement that the same code runs unchanged. Hedge and universal-quantifier lexicons may appear only as hints to the LLM. The number rule must earn verdict mode through self-validation, because derived totals, unit conversions and number words could false-fail hidden replies even though dev showed none. If `prepare()` calls the LLM at all, for example to build a checklist, it runs again for each scored run and could produce a different checklist each time. That turns into run-to-run inconsistency, so keep `prepare()` deterministic and LLM-free if possible.

Several popular techniques work against this metric. Long generated rubrics lowered sensitivity in FBI and contain about 40% harmful items. Extended thinking is slow and its output is invisible through the compat layer. Sampling at T>0 for self-consistency improves correlation with humans ([Yamauchi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13639v1)) but adds exactly the run-to-run variance that is scored. Optimizing the prompt with DSPy at runtime on a handful of examples overfits ([arXiv 2412.15298](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15298v1)). "Any sub-check fails → FAIL" aggregation over loosely scoped checks will push catch up and keep down. The harmonic mean punishes that. Finally, do not cite "Reliability without Validity" as evidence, since it has been withdrawn.

## Conclusion

The literature and our dev numbers point to the same conclusion. Haiku 4.5 is already among the most sensitive cheap judges; it fails on absences and weakened modality because nothing in the reply marks them. The fix is to change the question the model is asked, not the model. Asking "is this reply OK?" leaves the omission invisible. Asking "the policy rule you matched says X applies only if Y — does the reply keep Y?" makes it visible. Moving the verdict into code pays off twice: the same extracted fields that raise catch also make the verdict reproducible, because a field like "condition dropped" is more stable than a holistic PASS/FAIL token.

The biggest open uncertainty is the surprise criterion. Everything above is tuned on over-promising, where the policy works naturally as the list of facts to check against. If the 14:30 criterion is not policy-grounded, the generic parts of the design still help: the self-validating rules, the "is the reply's statement stronger than its reference" framing, examples with rationales, code-side aggregation and verify-on-fail. The policy-specific gains will shrink. That is why rehearsing a mock criterion that has nothing to do with numbers is the most valuable experiment to run before code freeze.
